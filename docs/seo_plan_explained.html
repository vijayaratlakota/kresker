2. Problem 1: we are telling the AIs to go away

Your robots.txt currently contains instructions blocking every major AI assistant. You did not write them. Cloudflare added them.

What I actually found

This is a real extract from kresker.com/robots.txt as it is served right now. I have removed the long legal preamble and kept the instructions:

User-agent: *
Content-Signal: search=yes, ai-train=no, use=reference

User-agent: GPTBot              Disallow: /     <-- ChatGPT
User-agent: ClaudeBot           Disallow: /     <-- Claude
User-agent: Google-Extended     Disallow: /     <-- Gemini
User-agent: CCBot               Disallow: /
User-agent: Bytespider          Disallow: /
User-agent: Applebot-Extended   Disallow: /
User-agent: Amazonbot           Disallow: /
User-agent: meta-externalagent  Disallow: /
Reading that. User-agent names a specific crawler. Disallow: / means “you may not read anything on this site” — the / is the whole website. So each line above is a door closed to one company's crawler.

Why this happened without you doing anything

Cloudflare has a feature called Managed robots.txt. When it is switched on, Cloudflare writes AI-blocking rules into your robots.txt automatically and keeps them updated. It exists for a good reason: many website owners are angry that AI companies read their content, train on it, and send no visitors back.

For a news site or a blog, that anger makes sense — their content is the product, and an AI that summarises it steals the visit. For you it is backwards. Your content is not the product; your dubbing tool is. You want ChatGPT to read your pages, because you want it to say “try Kresker”. Every one of those blocked lines is a recommendation you will never get.

The part that needs your judgement

“Let the AI read my site” is not one decision, it is three. The report you were given treats it as one switch. It is not, and the difference is money.

PermissionWhat it lets them doMy advice

AI input

And I checked every page. Home, pricing, about, contact, privacy, terms, disclaimer, plus made-up addresses that do not exist — every single one returns the identical 5,999 bytes. Byte for byte the same file.

Why this happens. Your website is built as a single-page application. That means the server sends one nearly-empty shell, and then JavaScript builds the real page inside the visitor's browser. It is a completely normal way to build a web app — and it is exactly right for your dashboard, where people upload videos and watch progress. The problem is that your marketing pages are built the same way, and marketing pages need to be readable by machines.

Why this is worse than it sounds

People assume Google runs JavaScript so it must be fine. Two things are wrong with that.

First, Google does run JavaScript, but reluctantly. It puts those pages in a separate, slower queue and revisits them less often. A page that needs code to run competes against millions of pages that do not. You are choosing the harder path for no benefit.

Second, and this is the important one: the AI crawlers do not run JavaScript at all. I tested this directly by pretending to be each of them:

Pretending to beGot backMeaning
Googlebot5,999 bytesSame empty shell as everyone.
GPTBot (ChatGPT)5,999 bytesCannot run code. Sees nothing.
ClaudeBot5,999 bytesCannot run code. Sees nothing.
PerplexityBot5,999 bytesCannot run code. Sees nothing.
The two problems together Problem 1 locks the AI crawlers out. Problem 2 means that even after we unlock the door, they walk in and find an empty room. Both have to be fixed, in that order, before any content work is worth doing.
Why the report you were given misses this The blueprint tells us to write an llms.txt summary file and add machine-readable product facts to the pages. Both are good ideas and both are in this plan. But it never asks whether the pages have any readable content to begin with. Writing product facts into a page that no crawler can read is decorating a house with no doors.

7.2 Google Search Console

Google Search Console is a free tool from Google that shows you your own site through Google's eyes: which pages it has read, which searches you appeared in, how many people clicked, and what is broken. Without it you are guessing. With it, every later decision is based on real numbers.

Set-up, in order:

  1. Go to search.google.com/search-console and add a property.
  2. Choose Domain, not URL prefix, and enter kresker.com. A domain property covers www, non-www, and every address in one place — otherwise you end up with four half-empty views of your own site.
  3. Verify by DNS. Google gives you a line of text to add to your domain settings; because your domain is managed at Cloudflare, this is a two-minute copy and paste.
  4. Submit your sitemap: https://kresker.com/sitemap.xml
  5. Use URL Inspection on the homepage, then click Test live URL and open the rendered HTML.
Do step 5 — it is the most educational five minutes available That screen shows you the page exactly as Google received it. You will see Problem 2 with Google's own eyes rather than taking my word for it. Keep the screenshot; when Phase 1 is done, take the same screenshot again and the difference is the proof it worked.

8. Phase 1 — make the website readable

This is the engineering work, and it is the one thing on the list that has to be done properly. Roughly a week.

The good news I found in your server

Your server is already configured in a way that makes this much cheaper than usual. Simplified, the rule it follows is:

if a real file exists at this address, send it
otherwise, send the empty shell and let JavaScript sort it out

Read that again, because it is the whole opportunity: if finished pages exist as real files, your server will serve them automatically. No new software, no new service, no change to how the site is hosted. We only have to produce the files.

Pre-rendering means: at the moment we build the website, run each public page once ourselves, wait for JavaScript to finish building it, and save the finished result as a plain file. The visitor then gets a complete page immediately, and the JavaScript takes over afterwards so everything still clicks and works. Same site, same code, better first delivery.

The options, in plain terms

8. Phase 1, continued — what else comes with it

Every page gets its own title and description

Right now all seven pages share one title. In a search result, the title is the blue line people click — so seven pages competing with one identical title means Google cannot tell them apart, and neither can a human scanning results.

What it does: gives each page its own headline and summary in search results.
Why we need it: Google uses the title heavily to decide what a page is about.
How it helps: your pricing page can rank for “video dubbing price” instead of competing with your own homepage.

Each page says which address is the real one

A canonical link is a line on the page saying “the official address for this page is X”. It matters because the same page can be reached many ways — with a trailing slash, with tracking codes on the end, with or without www. Without this line, Google may treat those as several competing pages and split the credit between them.

Share previews become per-page

Today, pasting a link to your pricing page anywhere shows a preview claiming to be the homepage. Small, but it makes every shared link slightly wrong.

One decision I will need from you

Web addresses can end with a slash or not: /pricing or /pricing/. Both work. What matters is picking one and using it everywhere — in the sitemap, in the canonical links, in every internal link. If we use both, Google sees two versions of every page and splits the credit.

I will bring you a recommendation with the trade-off. It is a two-minute decision, but it has to be made before Google indexes the pages, because changing it afterwards means asking Google to re-learn every address.

What Phase 1 does not change Your dashboard — uploading, dubbing, billing, admin — is untouched. It should stay exactly as it is built, because that part genuinely is an app and there is nothing for Google to index behind a login anyway. This work is only about the public pages.

9. Phase 2 — make it machine-readable

Now that pages can be read, we make them easy to understand. About one to two weeks, and this is where the AI recommendations actually come from.

9.1 A summary file written for AI

llms.txt is a plain summary of your product, in simple text, at kresker.com/llms.txt. It is a new convention that AI assistants increasingly look for. Where a human wants a nice page with pictures, an AI wants the facts with nothing in the way: what the product is, what it does, what it costs, which languages, what the limits are.

Two files: a short one, and a longer one with the complete detail for assistants that can read a lot at once.

What it does: hands AI assistants a clean, correct summary instead of making them guess from marketing copy.
Why we need it: it removes ambiguity. An assistant deciding whether to recommend you needs to know you support Telugu and cost ₹299. If it has to infer that, it may decline to state it — or state it wrongly.
How it helps: this is the single highest-value item for getting recommended, and most of your competitors have not done it either.

The one rule for this file, and it is not optional It must be generated from your live system, never typed by hand.

Your prices, plans, minute limits and language list all already exist in one place in your code. The file should be built from those, automatically. If it is hand-written, then the first time you change a price, the file starts lying — and it lies to every AI assistant that reads it, for as long as nobody notices.

This is exactly how the blueprint's version came to claim a free tier of “3 videos a month”. Hand-written facts drift. Generated facts cannot.

9.2 Product facts embedded in the pages

Structured data is a hidden block on the page that states plainly, in a format both Google and AI systems understand: this is an organisation called Kresker; it makes a web application; here are its prices; here are the questions people ask and the answers. Humans never see it. Machines read it first.

We would add blocks describing: your organisation, your website, the application itself with real prices in rupees, the navigation trail on each page, and a set of frequently asked questions.

What it does: converts your marketing pages into facts a machine can quote.

10. Phase 3 — languages, content, and the money problem

Six to ten weeks. But one business decision has to come first, or this phase buys traffic that cannot become customers.

Read this before spending anything on international SEO Your prices are in rupees only, and the price shown excludes 18% GST, an Indian tax.

Now picture the visitor this plan is designed to attract. Someone in Brazil searches in Portuguese, finds your page, likes it, clicks the price — and meets ₹999 plus 18% GST. Three options, and this is your decision, not a technical one:

  2. Show USD alongside rupees. Much less work, and removes most of the confusion — people can price a dollar even if they cannot price a rupee.
  3. Focus on India and Indian-language markets first. Genuinely valid: it is where your product is strongest, where competition is weakest, and where the checkout already works. Then expand once the money side is ready.
My honest view Option 3 first, then 1 or 2. Your 14 Indian languages are your real advantage, the payment system already fits that market, and you will see results faster there than by fighting for Spanish and German. International expansion is a good second move, not a good first one.

10. Phase 3, continued

Separate addresses per language

Language folders. The Spanish version of your site lives at kresker.com/es/, Hindi at kresker.com/hi/, and so on. This matters because it must be one website, not several: everything that builds your reputation with Google adds to the same domain. Putting Spanish on a separate address like es.kresker.com would split that reputation in half.
hreflang. A set of lines telling Google “this page has a Spanish version over here, a Hindi version over there”. Without it, Google may treat your six language versions as six copies of the same thing and pick one, or think you are duplicating content. With it, a Spanish searcher gets the Spanish page automatically.
The mistake to avoid: a translated shell over an English product Translating your landing page into Spanish while the dashboard, buttons and emails stay English produces a bad experience — someone is convinced in their language, then abandoned in yours. It also wastes the traffic we worked to get.

So a language market means: translated marketing pages and translated interface. That is why six markets is the right number and 43 is not.

Content: pages that answer real questions

You have seven pages. Nobody searches for any of them except your pricing. The traffic is in questions:

  • “how to add a Telugu audio track to a YouTube video” — your unique strength, almost no competition
  • “dub a video into Hindi free”
  • “how to translate a course video for Indian students”
  • “best way to reach non-English viewers on YouTube”
  • a page per language: what it costs, what it sounds like, how long it takes
The real risk in mass-producing pages The blueprint suggests generating 500+ pages from a template. Google actively penalises this under a policy on mass-produced content, updated in 2024 specifically to target it. Hundreds of near-identical pages with only the language name swapped is the exact pattern they look for.

The test each page must pass: does it contain something that could only exist on this page? A real voice sample in that language, real timings, a real example. If the only difference is a word substitution, it is a liability rather than an asset.

So: fewer, better pages. Thirty genuinely useful ones will outperform five hundred templated ones, and they cannot get you penalised.
