1. What we are building

A website where someone signs up, uploads a video, picks a language, and gets a dubbed video back.

You already own the hard part. The dubbing engine works, and you spent days tuning it to sound right. What is missing is everything around it: accounts, payments, storage, and a queue. That is what we are building, and it sits in front of your engine like a shop front in front of a workshop.

The shape of the whole system. The user only ever talks to the middle box. They can never reach your engine directly.
The single most important thing on this page The user's browser never talks to your dubbing engine, and never sees any password or key. It only talks to our new website. That one boundary is what keeps your engine safe and your keys private.

2. The one rule: never touch the dubbing engine

Your dub quality is your whole product. So the plan is built to make changing it impossible by accident.

Your engine has dozens of settings. You tested them for days and found the combination that sounds right. If the website could change any of them, one bad request could ruin a customer's video — and you would not know why.

So we do the opposite of flexible. The website is allowed to choose one thing only: the language. Everything else is fixed in our code as a locked list of settings.

The user picks a language. Our code fills in everything else from a fixed list. The engine cannot tell one customer's job from another.
Why "stamped with a fingerprint"? Every job records exactly which settings were used. So if a customer ever says "this dub is worse than last month's", you can check whether anything actually changed instead of guessing. It also means you can undo a bad change in one step.
Three traps we found by reading your engine's code Your engine has some unhelpful factory defaults. If we send nothing, it does not show an error — it quietly does the wrong thing:
If we forget...What the customer gets
to name the audio trackThe original video, undubbed. No error at all. This has already happened once on your project.
to set the timing modeAudio slowly drifts out of sync with the mouths
to set voice matchingA breathy hiss at the start of roughly 1 line in 3
This is exactly why the settings list is locked and written down, rather than left to defaults.

3. What happens when someone dubs a video

Nine steps, about 55 minutes for a 30-minute video. The user just waits, or closes the tab and gets an email.

Green circles are steps where the expensive box is switched on. Blue circles are cheap. Notice how few green steps there are.
What happens if the customer presses F5? Nothing. The job carries on. This is worth understanding, because it is the difference between your current setup and the website.

Today, when you dub a video yourself, your browser tab is doing part of the job — it holds the translated text in memory and hands it to the engine at the end. Press F5 and that text is gone, because the only copy was in the tab. About 12 minutes of work for a long video.

On the website, the browser only watches. The customer clicks once and the work happens on the server: our worker drives the box, and every result is written into our database as it appears. The tab is a window onto that, not a participant.

So on F5, this is the whole sequence: the page reloads, asks our server "how is job 1234 doing?", the server reads the database, and the progress bar comes back exactly where it was. The box never knew the browser went away.
And they will not only press F5 on purpose You are right that nobody deliberately refreshes a page they are waiting on. But over 17 to 55 minutes of rendering, plenty happens by itself: the wifi drops, a phone locks, a laptop sleeps, a tab crashes, they switch to their phone, or they simply close it and get on with their day.

That last one is the point. "Upload it and come back later" has to be a normal thing to do, not something that breaks the job. The customer should be able to shut the laptop, and get an email when it is ready. None of that is possible if the browser is holding anything important.

This is why the transcript and translation go into the database as each step finishes, and it is the main reason the backend exists at all rather than being a thin pass-through. Note that with transcript editing removed, F5 was never going to lose 12 minutes of the customer's work — there is nothing for them to edit. The database is there so their history, ownership, minutes and refunds survive, and so the box can be switched off without anything going dark.

4. How the video gets to them, cheaply

They can download their dub as many times as they like for 7 days, then it is deleted. Done the obvious way, that would cost you money every single time. Here is how it costs nothing.

"Egress" is the word for this. Amazon charges you a fee every time a file leaves their network. Uploading to them is free; downloading from them is not. About ₹3.30 for a 30-minute video. That sounds tiny until you promise unlimited downloads.

If the video sits in Amazon's S3 and a customer downloads it ten times over the month, you pay that fee ten times. One enthusiastic customer could cost you more in fees than they pay you in subscription. So we move the finished video out of Amazon to Cloudflare R2, which charges nothing for downloads, ever.

The video only has to leave Amazon once. After that it lives somewhere that does not charge for downloads.
One correction to what you asked for You asked to use Cloudflare Workers to send the video from the GPU or from S3. A Worker on its own would not save anything — if it fetched the file from S3 each time, Amazon would still charge its fee, plus you would now also pay Cloudflare. It would be worse than doing nothing.

The saving comes from where the file lives, not from what serves it. So the plan is: put the finished video in Cloudflare R2, and use a Worker as the doorman that checks the person is allowed to have it. The Worker is still there — it just is not the part that saves the money.

And we must never send downloads from the GPU box. The box would have to be switched on for every download, at ₹47 an hour, which would destroy the whole money-saving switch in section 5.

Making sure only the right person can download

The storage is completely private. There is no public link to anybody's video. So how does a download work without letting strangers in? With a ticket.

The doorman never decides who is allowed in. It only checks a decision your website already made.
A bonus you get for free Cloudflare has servers all over the world, so it sends the video from somewhere near the customer rather than from Mumbai. An overseas customer gets a noticeably faster download. Since you are planning to sell internationally, that matters — and it is unusual for the cheaper option to also be the faster one.
And there is no editing any more An earlier version of this plan let paying customers check and correct the transcript and translation before rendering. That is removed. Every job is now one click, start to finish.

It removed a lot: a whole pause-and-continue system, five extra screens' worth of backend, and the riskiest untested assumption in the plan (that a half-done job could survive the box being switched off). It also saved about a week of building.

What you give up: roughly 1 line in 4 is not placed perfectly, and a customer with a wrong name or brand in their dub now has no way to fix it themselves. They either accept it or ask you. That is a real trade — worth revisiting later if customers actually ask for it, rather than guessing now.

5. The money-saving switch

The box costs ₹47 an hour while it is on. Leaving it on all month would cost ₹34,000. So it switches itself on and off.

The checker refuses to switch off if any real work is happening. Failing to switch off wastes money; switching off wrongly destroys a customer's video. So it errs towards wasting a little money.
The box switches on at UPLOAD - and we do not try to save money here The moment a customer starts uploading, the box begins waking up. Not after the upload finishes, not when translation starts. The six-minute warm-up happens while their video is still going up, so by the time the file has landed the machine is ready and work starts straight away.

Why not wait and save a little? Because the six minutes get paid either way — starting early mostly just moves the warm-up somewhere the customer cannot see it. The only time it costs extra is when someone's upload is slower than the warm-up, and the box sits ready for a few minutes waiting. That is a few rupees.

Those few rupees are the whole product. The alternative is every customer staring at a six-minute spinner after they have already waited for their upload, with nothing visibly happening. That is what makes software feel broken. We save money on the switch-off (below), never on the switch-on.
Why 10 minutes and not 5 Every start-up costs about 6 minutes of paid time before any real work happens, whether the video is 60 seconds or 30 minutes. So switching off just before the next customer arrives is the most wasteful thing the system can do.

Waiting 5 extra minutes costs about ₹4. An unnecessary restart costs about ₹5 and makes the next customer wait two extra minutes. So 10 minutes is actually cheaper than 5 any time a second customer shows up within that window. It only costs more on a genuinely quiet day.
"Nothing is lost when it switches off" — switching off an Amazon machine is like closing a laptop lid, not like formatting it. The hard disk keeps everything, so nothing in progress is damaged and the next job starts from a clean, intact machine.

6. Where everything is stored

Three places, three very different lifetimes. Getting this right is what makes the history page honest.

Text is cheap and kept forever. Video is kept 7 days in Cloudflare, where downloads are free, then deleted. Scratch files are wiped immediately.
What this means on their history page They always see every video they ever dubbed, with the transcript still readable, because that is text and we never delete it. After 7 days the download button stops working, because the file is gone. For those 7 days they can download it as often as they like, at no cost to you.

The row stays and the button greys out with a reason. That is more honest than deleting the history so that every button always works.
Why the video is deleted after a week The honest reason is privacy, not money. Once a customer has collected their dub, keeping a copy of their video on your servers stops being a service and starts being a risk. Every extra day it sits there is one more day it could leak, one more thing you would have to hand over if somebody asked, and one more thing to explain. Deleting it is the safer default.

It does happen to save about ₹170 a month in storage, but that is a bonus, not the reason.

Three things make this fair rather than annoying:
  • You tell them the first time they download. A message they have to click past, with the real date on it — "this video is deleted on 31 August at 18:40" — not a vague "in 7 days". A date reads as a fact; a duration reads as a suggestion.
  • A small countdown sits next to the video every time they visit: "deleted in 6d 4h". In the last day it switches to hours and minutes and changes colour. Nobody is caught out.
  • They can delete it themselves, sooner. If a customer wants their video gone today, that should be one button, not an email to support. It cannot be undone, so it asks once first.
One rule that protects you here Deleting a video must not give the customer their minutes back. The minutes paid for the work of making the dub, and that work already happened.

If deleting returned the minutes, anyone could dub a video, download it, delete it, and do it again forever — a paid plan with no limit. Easy to miss, expensive to discover.
And the box needs a bigger disk It is currently 79% full — 32 GB free out of 146 GB. That is enough for the dubbing itself, but uncomfortable, because a completely full disk fails every job rather than one. You chose to grow it to 250 GB, which costs about ₹803 more per month and takes free space to about 132 GB. Four times the breathing room.

7. Keeping it safe

You asked that no key, no password, and nobody else's data can ever leak into the browser. Here is the shape of that.

The browser is treated as hostile. Everything valuable lives behind the wall.
Why the "15-minute ticket" instead of just a link? The video warehouse is completely private — there are no public links. When a user wants their video, our server signs a temporary ticket for that one file. It stops working after 15 minutes. So even if the link leaks, it is useless very quickly, and nobody can guess their way to somebody else's video.
A customer never touches the dubbing box. Not once. This is worth being very clear about, because it is easy to assume otherwise. The engine on the GPU box has no password on it at all — anyone who can reach it gets in, and that machine holds keys that can spend money on your Amazon account. So it is put somewhere the internet simply cannot reach: 
customer's browser  --->  our website        (public, needs login)
our website         --->  our worker         (private)
our worker          --->  the dubbing box    (private, no public address at all)
Three hops, and the customer only ever talks to the first one. There is no request they can make that arrives at the box. The only two things they touch are our website and the download link (which comes from Cloudflare, not from the box).
So how does their transcript reach their dashboard? We copy it out as it is made. The box is a workshop, not a filing cabinet. As each step finishes, our worker takes the result out of the box and writes it into our own database:
  • after transcribing → every line of the original text, with its timings and who said it
  • after translating → every line of the translated text
  • after rendering → the finished video is pushed straight to Cloudflare storage
  • then the box's copies are deleted
So when the customer opens their dashboard, it reads from our database and Cloudflare. The box could be switched off, or replaced with a brand new one, and their history page would look exactly the same.
"But switching the box off doesn't erase its disk, does it?" — correct, it does not. The files would still be sitting there. The problem is different: the box is switched off most of the day, so a dashboard that read from it would be dead most of the day, and waking a ₹47-an-hour graphics computer just to display a line of text is absurd. On top of that, its disk is already 79% full and filling by itself, you cannot run proper searches across it, and it would not come with you if you ever moved Amazon accounts. Our database has none of those problems.

8. How people pay you

Razorpay handles the card and UPI details. They never touch your servers, which keeps you out of a lot of legal complexity.

The truth about money comes from Razorpay's own signed message, never from the customer's computer.
Something you need to know about your annual Pro price Indian rules cap any automatic repeating payment at ₹15,000 per charge unless the customer personally approves each one. This applies to card auto-payments as well as UPI, not just UPI. Your annual prices at 20% off work out as:
Starter₹2,870fine
Creator₹9,590fine
Pro₹14,390only ₹610 under the limit
It works today. But if you ever raise Pro above about ₹1,562 a month, that yearly charge would start needing the customer to approve it every single time — and an approval nobody is watching for is a renewal that quietly fails. You would lose the customer without either of you deciding anything.

DECIDED: annual plans are taken as a one-off payment rather than a repeating auto-payment. The cap only applies to auto-payments, so this removes the problem completely, at any price you might charge later. Monthly plans keep using auto-payment — at ₹1,499 they are nowhere near the limit and never will be.
The one catch, so it is not a surprise later A one-off payment does not renew itself. Nothing charges the customer when their year runs out.

So the reminder emails stop being a nicety and become the thing that keeps yearly customers: 14 days before, 3 days before, and on the day itself, each with a link straight to checkout. Give them a 7-day grace period too, so somebody who pays a day late does not lose their history or a video mid-job.

Without those reminders a yearly customer does not cancel — they simply vanish, and you find out from a chart months later.
Accepting foreign cards takes time, so start now Razorpay does not switch on international cards by default. You have to request it, pass their checks, and their own documentation says activating a payment method takes around 10 working days on average.

That is waiting, not work. Apply for it now, while the building happens, so it is ready when you launch. If it is not approved in time, launch India-only — the plan stores prices in a way that lets you add dollar pricing later without rebuilding anything.

9. Your admin panel

One private page, only you can open it, that answers the questions you will actually ask and does the few jobs you should never do by hand.

What you wantWhat the panel shows or does
Is the box on or off?The real answer, asked of Amazon directly — not what our own database thinks. Plus a stop button as a safety valve.
Who is logged in?Everyone signed in, when they signed in, and from where. You can also sign someone out.
Has this person subscribed?Their plan, whether it is still active, when it ends, and how many minutes they have left.
Give someone a subscriptionSearch for the person, pick a plan and how many months, type a reason. Done.
How much have I earned?Today, this week, and total — counting money that actually arrived, with refunds taken off.
Someone emailed to say their dub failed but their credits were takenTheir videos by name, whether each finished or failed, how many minutes it cost them, and a refund button for that one video.
Dub videos yourselfUnlimited, no quota, through the same pipeline.
Everything you change is written downWho did what, to whom, when, and why. Permanently.
Three rules that keep the numbers honest 1. A refund adds a line, it never rewrites one. When you refund someone's minutes we do not go back and edit the original charge — we add a second line that cancels it out. The balance ends up correct and the story still adds up when you read it a year later. Editing the past makes the total look right and the history a lie.

2. Giving a free subscription must not look like a sale. It would be easy to record a gifted plan as though money came in — and then the earnings figure on that very same screen is wrong. A gift is recorded as a gift. The earnings number only ever counts money Razorpay actually took.

3. Your own test dubs get labelled as yours. They cost real GPU money, so they appear in your costs. But they stay out of anything that looks like customer demand, otherwise you end up measuring your own testing and mistaking it for business.
This is the most dangerous page in the whole system Everywhere else the website can only fetch your own data — that is the rule that stops one customer seeing another's videos. The admin panel is the one deliberate exception: it looks up other people's accounts on purpose. So it is treated accordingly:
  • The panel is not part of the website customers download. Not hidden, not greyed out — simply absent from their copy.
  • Every request re-checks that you are an admin, on the server. Never trusted from the browser.
  • Giving subscriptions and giving refunds ask for your password again, because those two move money. If someone ever steals your signed-in session, they still cannot quietly hand out a year of Pro.
"Why bother writing down every change?" — because in six months somebody, possibly you, will ask "why does this account have Pro without ever paying?" or "why does this person's balance not match what they were charged?". If the panel keeps a permanent note of every action and the reason you typed at the time, both are answered in seconds. Without it, neither can be answered at all.

10. What it costs to run

Two kinds of cost: a fixed monthly bill you pay even with zero customers, and a small cost per video.

The margins per customer are healthy. The fixed bill is what needs the first few customers.
The number that matters most You break even at roughly 5–7 Pro subscribers, or 25–30 Starter subscribers.

After that, each new customer is nearly all profit, because they only cost you ₹20–340 of the ₹299–1,499 they pay.
Watch the free tier - it is the most expensive thing per minute A 60-second free trial needs about 6 minutes of warm-up for 1.6 minutes of actual work. Warm-up is nearly 80% of the cost. So a free trial costs you around ₹6–7 — while a 30-minute paid video, thirty times longer, costs ₹53–86.

A thousand trials would cost about ₹6,500, close to your whole fixed monthly bill. The way to keep that under control is to limit how often someone can have one, not how fast they get it:
  • One 60-second trial per account, for life — not 90 seconds every month. A monthly freebie is an invitation to make throwaway email addresses, and it turns a one-time cost into one you pay for that person forever.
  • The trial starts the box straight away, just like a paid job. No waiting in a separate queue.
Why the trial must not be made to wait It is tempting to save the warm-up by having free videos ride along with a paying customer's job and telling people "your free preview will be ready in a few hours". That saves about ₹5 per trial, and it is a bad trade.

Think about who is using the trial. It is somebody deciding whether to pay you. They will not come back hours later to check a preview — they will close the tab and try someone else, and you will never see that you lost them. On top of that, the most convincing thing about your product is that one click gives a finished dub in minutes. Making the trial slow hides exactly the thing you most want people to see.

The better way to think about the ₹6–7: it is not a render cost, it is the price of getting a customer, paid once. Compare it to a ₹299 subscription, not to the cost of a paid video. Even if only 1 in 20 people who try it subscribe, you are well ahead.
You still get the saving, just without the delay If a trial happens to arrive while the box is already awake — a paying customer is mid-job, or another trial came in during the ten-minute idle window — the warm-up is already paid for and that trial costs only about ₹1–2. Once you have real traffic this happens a lot on its own. You take the discount when it turns up, instead of forcing it by making people queue.

11. How long it takes to build

About 7 to 10 weeks of full-time work for one developer, to something you would happily charge money for.

One developer, one task at a time. The dashed line is the point where a real video goes in and a real dubbed video comes out, for a real logged-in user.
Why the first item is not code Before writing anything, one short session is needed with your box switched on, recording exactly what your engine is asked and what it answers. Reading the code got most of it, but one important thing is still unclear: whether your good dubs came from your engine doing the translating, or from Gemini doing it outside the engine. Those give completely different results.

Guessing would mean building the wrong thing and rewriting it. Half a day of checking prevents that.
The order is deliberate The one-click path is built first and completely: uploading, dubbing, storing and delivering one video end to end, for one logged-in user, before anything about money or polish. That way there is always something real working, and everything afterwards is an addition to something proven rather than a complication in something half-built.

12. How a dub actually flows, and three things still to sort out

The five steps, and they do not change The customer picks a language and presses one button. Behind that, the website asks your existing engine to do exactly what it already does:
  1. Upload and prepare — the video goes to the box, the audio is pulled out, and the singing/music is separated from the speech.
  2. Transcribe — Gemini writes down what was said, Google Chirp says exactly when each word happened, and the engine works out who was speaking and takes a voice sample of each person.
  3. Translate — Gemini translates, 20 lines at a time, told how long each line is allowed to be so it fits the gap it has to sit in.
  4. Render — each line is spoken in that character's cloned voice and fitted to its slot.
  5. Deliver — the dub is laid over the original video and the finished file goes to storage, then to the customer's dashboard.
Steps 1, 2 and 4 need the expensive box. Step 3 does not, but it sits between two that do, so the box stays on for the whole job — switching it off and on again would cost more than it saved.

Two need a decision from you. The third is now answered.

The whole plan in five sentences
  1. Build a normal, well-secured website in front of your dubbing engine, and treat the engine as a sealed box that we switch on, feed, read from, and switch off.
  2. The website can choose the language and nothing else, so your dub quality cannot drift and nobody can meddle with the engine through it.
  3. Text goes in the database and is kept forever; the dubbed video goes to Cloudflare R2 and is kept 7 days, where the customer can download it unlimited times at no cost to you, and is then deleted for their privacy; scratch files are wiped after every job.
  4. The expensive box switches itself on when someone uploads and off 10 minutes after the last job, having first checked that nothing is transcribing, translating or rendering.
  5. No key, no password and nobody else's data ever reaches the browser, and every question asked of the database carries "and this belongs to the person logged in" inside it.
