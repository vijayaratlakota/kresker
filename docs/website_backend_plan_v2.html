2. The golden preset: how "one click, defaults except language" is enforced

You asked for a single click, with every option at its tuned default and only the target language chosen by the user. That requirement and the "do not disturb the pipeline" requirement are the same requirement, and they are satisfied by the same mechanism.

2.1 The mechanism

The API accepts exactly two pipeline-relevant inputs from a client: target_lang, and optionally source_lang when the user overrides auto-detection. Everything else is a constant in the worker's source, loaded from a versioned preset file. There is no request field, query parameter, header, cookie or database column through which a browser can change a pipeline setting. An unknown key in a job creation request is rejected, not ignored.

Two things follow. First, the output cannot drift because of the website, since the website has no vocabulary for expressing a different configuration. Second, the engine cannot be manipulated through our API, which removes a whole class of attack: no client can ask the GPU box to run an expensive quality tier, disable a guard, or point a path somewhere it should not go.

2.4 The three traps

1. default_track defaults to "original". Omitting it does not error. The endpoint returns the video with its original audio, and this has already shipped once: a delivered file measured byte-identical to the source while every artifact reported a clean run. The worker must refuse to download a job that reports no rendered track, rather than passing the original off as a dub, and must verify the downloaded file's audio track language before marking the job done.

2. timing_strategy defaults to "concise", and smart_fit is worse than either. The tuned value is strict_slot. With smart_fit plus a non-zero overflow budget, per-line overruns accumulate so every later line lands after the mouth that speaks it; with allow_video_retime the renderer is permitted to slow the picture down to accommodate long audio. Together those are the reported "video going fast, audio slow" drift.

3. voice_match defaults to "per_line". The tuned value is consistent. A per-line reference is cut from that line's own separated vocals, so it carries separation residue that the cloner reproduces as a breathy hiss at the line's onset: measured at 5 of 15 renders for per_line against 1 of 15 for consistent.

2.5 What the worker must never do

NeverWhy
Set any OMNIVOICE_* environment variable on the containerThe container's environment is fixed at create time and holds only three variables. Adding one permanently requires docker rm, which destroys the in-place-patched backend. Every tuned value is a module default and must stay one.
Reload or restart the app while a render is runningModule defaults are read once at import. A reload mid-render abandons the job and leaves half-written segment files.
Run two jobs at once on the boxOne GPU, one pinned thread. The queue serialises strictly; section 7 does this with FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED.

Leave HF_TOKEN unattendedWithout a valid token, diarization degrades silently to a gap heuristic that merges every character into one voice with no cloning. The token needs an expiry alarm, not just a value.
Introduce any identifier containing leadinA guarded invariant counts occurrences in dub_generate.py and must stay at 5. Name things onset, snap, run, bridge.

2.6 The preset is versioned, hashed, and recorded per job

The preset lives in one file, carries a version string, and the worker writes both the version and a SHA-256 of the exact payload it sent into the job's row. Three things this buys, all of which matter more than they look:

  • Provenance. When a customer says a dub is worse than last month's, you can prove whether the configuration changed, rather than arguing about it.
  • Rollback. A preset change is a one-line revert with an audit trail, not an archaeology exercise.
  • A regression test with teeth. One known-good source video, rendered nightly, compared against a stored reference. The pipeline is your entire product advantage; it deserves a canary that shouts before a customer does.

The per-video cap has to be min(30, minutes_left), not a flat 30. Starter's whole monthly allowance is 10 minutes, so a Starter subscriber can never dub anything longer than 10 minutes however the limit is written. 3.2 Quota is a ledger, not a counter

minutes_used  = SUM(usage_ledger.minutes_charged) since current_period_start
minutes_left  = plan.minutes_per_period - minutes_used

Billing is on video duration as measured by ffprobe, decided before any work starts, so the user knows the cost in advance and a slow render never costs them more. Checked twice: when the upload URL is requested, so a large upload fails early, and again at job creation, because time passes. A failed job writes no usage row — nobody pays for our bugs. A ledger rather than a decrementing counter because a counter cannot be reconciled when something half-fails, and cannot be explained to a customer who disputes a charge.

Retention applies to files, never to the record. Rendered videos are kept 7 days for subscribers and 24 hours on free; source videos are deleted as soon as the job ends, because nothing re-runs from them. The job row, transcript and translation are kept forever, so the user's history stays complete even after a file is gone. Full breakdown in section 5.6, and the deletion machinery in 5.8. Less stored media is less to leak and a smaller bill; the numbers live in the plans table so they change without a deployment.

3.4 The free tier is the expensive one, per minute

A 60-second free trial needs about 6 minutes of boot for 1.6 minutes of work. Boot is nearly 80% of the bill, so a trial costs roughly ₹6–7 — against ₹53–86 for a 30-minute paid job that is thirty times longer. A thousand trials is about ₹6,500, close to a full month of fixed infrastructure.

The way to bound that is lifetime scope, not delay:
  1. One 60-second trial per verified account, for life — not 90 seconds every month. A recurring free allowance is a standing invitation to churn through disposable email addresses, and it turns a one-off acquisition cost into a subscription we pay on the user's behalf indefinitely. Lifetime scope makes the spend finite and predictable per person.
  2. The trial runs immediately, starting a box if none is warm — same queue, same priority and same wait as a paying job. No separate free lane.
Do not make free jobs wait for a paying job to bring a box up. It saves about ₹5 and it costs the customer.

The trial is the only chance to prove the output is worth paying for. Somebody deciding whether to subscribe will not come back in a few hours to look at a preview — they will close the tab and try a competitor, and that abandonment never shows up in the metrics as a lost sale. Worse, a queued trial hides the single most persuasive property of the whole product: that one click turns a video into a finished dub in minutes with nothing to configure. Concealing that from every prospective customer to save five rupees inverts the entire purpose of having a free tier.

The right way to book it is customer acquisition cost, paid once per person, judged against a ₹299 Starter subscription rather than against the cost of a paid render. On that framing even a 5% conversion rate returns roughly twenty times the spend, and the trial has to be fast for the conversion to happen at all.
Batching still helps — as upside, not as policy. When a trial lands while a box is already warm, because a paying job is running or another trial arrived inside the ten-minute idle window, the boot is already paid for and the marginal cost drops to about ₹1–2. At any real volume that will happen often, and the ten-minute shutdown delay in section 6.2 makes bursts of trials share one boot with nobody being asked to wait. The difference is that we take the saving when it appears rather than manufacturing it by holding people in a queue.
Because a trial now starts a real boot, verification is the only thing bounding abuse. Require a verified email before the first free job runs. Keep a hard daily ceiling on free jobs across all accounts so a bad day is bounded regardless. Rate-limit new-account creation per IP and per device. Store the lifetime entitlement as a flag on the user row and never on a period counter — the entire point is that it does not reset, so it must not live in anything that current_period_start can roll over.

4. Delivering the video: Cloudflare R2 and a Worker

The finished video has to reach the user, on demand, as many times as they like, for thirty days. Doing that from S3 is the single most expensive recurring line item in the whole design, because AWS charges for every gigabyte that leaves it. This section removes that charge almost entirely.

4.1 One correction to the brief, and it is the whole saving

A Cloudflare Worker in front of S3 saves nothing. AWS bills egress on every byte that leaves S3 — including bytes going to Cloudflare. Putting a Worker in the middle means paying S3 egress and Cloudflare request charges, which is worse than serving from S3 directly.

The saving comes from where the file lives, not from what serves it. Cloudflare R2 charges nothing for egress, ever, including through the Workers API. So the design is: put the finished video in R2, and use a Worker to check permission and stream it. The Worker is the doorman; R2 is the reason the bill drops.

And never serve downloads from the GPU box. That was the other option in the brief and it is the worst of the three: the box would have to be running for every download — ₹47 an hour, defeating the entire auto-shutdown design in section 6 — it would still pay AWS egress per download, and it would expose a machine that has no authentication of any kind. Downloads are served from R2 and only from R2.

4.2 Where each byte goes, and what it costs

The important insight is that a video only has to leave AWS once. After that it lives outside AWS and can be served for free forever.

At your scale the operations are effectively free: one write per video and one read per download, against a million and ten million free. Storage is the only real line. 500 videos a month at 350 MB, each kept 7 days, is about 41 GB-month — roughly ₹40 a month after the free allowance.

For comparison, the same traffic held for 30 days was about 170 GB-month and ₹210. So the shorter window takes roughly ₹170 a month off the bill — worth having, but it is a side effect. The reason for 7 days is that holding a customer's video longer than they need it is a liability, not a feature (5.8).

Do not use the Infrequent Access storage class here, even though it looks cheaper. It is $0.01/GB instead of $0.015, but it adds a $0.01/GB retrieval fee and a 30-day minimum storage duration. A retrieval fee is a per-download charge by another name, so it would quietly undo the thing this entire section exists to achieve.

The minimum duration is now the bigger objection. Against a 7-day retention window you would be billed 30 days of storage for every object deleted on day 7 — over four times the storage you actually use, and that is before the per-download fee. The shorter the retention, the worse Infrequent Access gets. Standard storage, no retrieval fee, unlimited reads.

4.4 S3 is dropped entirely

Once outputs live in R2, there is nothing left for S3 to do. Uploads can go straight to R2 as well, using its S3-compatible presigned URLs, and the box pulls the source from R2 for free. That removes a whole AWS service from the design.

5. The job state machine

5.1 There is only one path

queued -> preparing -> transcribing -> translating -> rendering -> exporting -> done
                                                                              \-> failed
                                                                              \-> cancelled

No human input after the upload. The user picks a language, presses one button, and either watches live progress or closes the tab and gets an email. Every job is identical in shape, which is what makes the whole system predictable.

5.2 What removing editing bought

This is the largest simplification in the document, and it removes more than a feature. Earlier drafts let paying users check and correct the transcript, the translation and the speaker assignments before rendering. That is gone: no editing of any kind.

Without editing, a customer whose video has a few wrong lines — usually a name, a brand or industry jargon, which no translator handles reliably — has no way to fix it themselves. Their only options are to accept it or ask you. That is a real trade, and it is the right one for launching: the product stays simple, and if customers ask for editing you will have the evidence to justify building it properly rather than guessing now.

5.4 The row-identity rule still applies to the worker

Removing editing removes the product risk here, not the engineering one. VoiceStudio maps a render request onto its own stored segments by position: 
job["seg_order"] = [seg_ids[k] if k < len(seg_ids) else f"seg_{k}"
                    for k in range(len(req.segments))]
So sending a different number of rows than the box has stored shifts every later line onto a different slot. This has already happened on this project — 140 rows sent against 144 stored, and the finished video had the original Hindi speaking underneath the Telugu, because a model asked to fill a slot far longer than its words fills the surplus with the reference audio it was conditioned on.

So the worker must, without exception:
  • Send every row, in the original order, including any with empty text.
  • Always send segment_ids, so the mapping is explicit by stable id rather than implicit by position.
  • Assert the count before sending. If the number of rows it is about to send does not match the number the box reported at transcription, it fails the job loudly rather than rendering something wrong. Cheap, and it catches the class of bug that has already cost this project once.

5.5 The user's library

Every job a user starts belongs to them permanently and appears in their dashboard — finished, failed or cancelled. The tables behind it are in section 7.

5.8 Seven-day deletion, and the machinery behind it

DECISION: a dubbed video is deleted seven days after it is rendered. Downloads are unlimited inside that window. The user is told the deletion date on their first download, a countdown sits beside the video on the dashboard, and they can delete it sooner themselves. Free-tier outputs use the same machinery with a 24-hour window.

The reason is privacy, not cost. Holding a customer's video after they have collected it is a liability that grows quietly — every extra day is more material to leak, more to hand over if someone asks, and more to explain. The ₹170 a month it happens to save (4.3) is a side effect worth having, not the argument for doing it.

6. GPU on demand

This is the part that decides the bill, and the part where a bug is most expensive: either money burned on an idle GPU, or a paid render killed halfway.

6.1 The rule

START  when a job needs the box and no instance is running.
       Triggered when an upload URL is issued, so the box warms up
       while the video is still uploading.

STOP   when ALL of these are true:
         - no job is in preparing, transcribing, translating,
           rendering or exporting
         - no job is queued
         - no job is mid-upload (a presigned upload issued in the
           last 20 minutes and not yet turned into a job)
         - the last job step finished at least 10 minutes ago
         - the instance is actually running

Checked every minute by an EventBridge rule invoking a small Lambda, which reads the database and calls StopInstances. It lives outside the API deliberately, so an API crash cannot leave the GPU running. Because stopping requires the queue to be empty as well as idle, a burst of uploads keeps the box up and processes them back to back — faster for users and cheaper than starting and stopping repeatedly.

The mid-upload condition matters more than it looks. A user on a slow connection uploading a 30-minute video can take many minutes, during which no job exists yet and the queue is empty. Without that check the box could start on the first byte, shut down before the upload finished, and pay a second boot when the job finally arrived. Tracking issued upload URLs closes the gap.

With editing removed, the box is never handed back and forth. It is busy for one continuous stretch per job and then released. There is no pausing mid-job, nothing waits on a person, and no render has to survive an instance stop. That last point deleted the riskiest unverified assumption in this plan — see 5.2.
DECISION: the GPU comes up at the upload stage, and this is not a place to save money. The instance starts the moment an upload URL is issued — before the first byte arrives — so the six-minute warm-up runs while the customer's video is still going up. Not after the upload completes, not at transcribe, and never part-way through on a progress threshold.

The arithmetic, so the trade is on the record. Boot costs about six minutes of GPU time whenever it happens, so starting early usually costs nothing extra — it just moves the warm-up under the upload instead of after it. The one case where it does cost more is an upload slower than the boot: the box finishes warming and then idles until the file lands, and that idle is billed. On a slow connection that is a few rupees.

Those few rupees buy the thing the customer is actually paying for. The alternative is a six-minute spinner after they have already waited for their upload, on every single job, with nothing happening that they can see. That is the difference between a product that feels instant and one that feels broken, and it is not worth trading for small change.

Explicitly closed, so nobody reopens it as an optimisation:
  • Do not delay the start to trim idle minutes.
  • Do not gate the boot on upload progress or an estimated remaining time.
  • Do not stop the box between stages, including around translate (16.1.1).
Each of those saves a few rupees per job and costs user-visible waiting. The place to control GPU spend is the idle shutdown after work finishes (6.2), not the start.
The downside is already guarded. The no job is mid-upload condition in the STOP rule is what stops a slow upload turning into a shutdown and a second boot — which would cost more than the idle time it was trying to avoid. That guard is the reason starting this early is safe.

6.2 On 10 minutes rather than 5

Ten is the right number, and not just for comfort. Every boot costs about 6 minutes of GPU time whether the video is 60 seconds or 30 minutes, so a shutdown that happens just before the next job arrives is the most expensive thing the watchdog can do.

Holding the box 5 minutes longer costs about ₹4. A needless extra boot costs about ₹5 and makes a user wait two more minutes. So 10 minutes is cheaper than 5 whenever a second job arrives within that window, and only more expensive on a genuinely quiet day — roughly ₹1,200 a month at the worst case of ten isolated boots a day. Put the number in configuration, not in code, so it can be retuned from real arrival data once you have some.

6.3 The safety rules

  • Never stop with work in flight. The Lambda refuses if any job is in a working state. A 30-minute video's render is roughly 17 minutes; killing it wastes the job and the money.
  • If the database is unreachable, do nothing and raise an alarm. Failing to stop costs money; stopping wrongly destroys a paying customer's job. The failure mode is deliberately the expensive-but-safe one.
  • A stuck job must not pin the GPU. Any job in a working state longer than 90 minutes — well clear of a 30-minute video's honest worst case — is failed, the user is told, no quota is charged, and an alert fires.
  • A cost alarm independent of all of it. A CloudWatch alarm on instance running-hours per day emails you regardless of what the software believes is true.
  • Never run docker stop ourselves. See 6.4. Stopping the instance is the correct action and delivers SIGTERM properly.
  • Never serve a download from the box. It would have to be running for every download. Section 4.1.

6.4 The container does not always come back on boot

The project's own scripts disagree about this. start_box.ps1 says VoiceStudio "comes back on its own"; both start_instance.ps1 and _boot_voicestudio.sh say it "does NOT come back on boot" and start it explicitly. Both are correct, under different conditions, and the difference is exactly the one that matters here: 
restart=unless-stopped

  container was RUNNING when the instance stopped   -> comes back automatically
  container was explicitly `docker stop`-ped first  -> stays down across every boot
So an automated instance start can perfectly well produce a healthy box with no application listening on port 3900, and a worker that only polls /sysinfo would wait until its timeout and fail a paid job for no real reason.

The worker therefore needs a bounded recovery step, not just a readiness poll: if /sysinfo has not answered within a grace period, run docker start on the container over SSM — never docker stop, never docker rm — then resume polling, and only then give up. This also means the automated lifecycle must never explicitly stop the container, because doing so once poisons every subsequent boot.

6.5 What the user sees

Honest progress rather than a spinner that lies: "Starting the dubbing engine, about 2 minutes", then real percentages from the engine's own progress stream. For a 30-minute video the whole thing is roughly 55 minutes, so the flow is "we will email you when it is ready", with the page updating live if they stay on it. One email per job, at the end.

8.2.2 The HuggingFace token — OPEN, and it needs care

A full sweep of all 3,500 tracked files for nine classes of secret found one more real leak: _goldstate.txt line 19 carries HF_TOKEN=hf_… (37 characters), committed. The file is the output of _goldstate.sh, which dumps the container's environment — so it is a credential file whose name gives no hint of it. It is now untracked and ignored, and the value is unchanged.

Why this one is harder than the Gemini key. The HF token is in active use: it lives in the container's environment and diarization depends on it. Rotating it therefore means changing that environment — and the container's environment is fixed at create time, so a permanent change requires docker rm and a recreate. That is the one operation the handbook warns destroys the in-place-patched backend if done carelessly. _recreate_with_hf.sh exists to do exactly this, so the path is known, but it must be run deliberately, against a verified backup, with the five pinned MD5s and both guarded invariants re-checked afterwards.

Compounding it: if the token is merely invalidated and not correctly replaced, diarization does not fail loudly. It silently degrades to a silence-gap heuristic that merges every character into one speaker with no cloning — which looks like a quality regression, not a credential problem. That is the failure this project has already seen once.

My recommendation: rotate it as a planned maintenance step, not now — pair it with the next container recreate, and add the HF token expiry alarm from section 8.1 at the same time. I have not touched it.

8.4 Authorization, and the rule that keeps users apart

Every query that touches user data is scoped by the session's user id inside the query itself. 
SELECT * FROM jobs WHERE id = :job_id AND user_id = :session_user_id
Not "fetch it, then compare" — that pattern is how one forgotten if becomes a data breach. Job ids are UUIDs so they cannot be enumerated, but that is a second layer, never the defence. A job that does not belong to the caller returns 404, not 403, so the API does not confirm that someone else's job exists. Admin endpoints live under a separate path with a separate role check and write to audit_log every time.

That is acceptable today only because the security group allows exactly one home IP address. A public website changes the picture completely.
  • The instance gets no public IP and sits in a private subnet.
  • Port 3900 is allowed only from the worker's security group — not from an IP range, and not from the internet.
  • Its IAM role is narrowed to what the pipeline genuinely needs, and the failed policy update noted in v1 is fixed before anything depends on it.
  • No endpoint on our API proxies to it, and no endpoint accepts a URL or file path from a client and fetches it. The first would expose an unauthenticated engine to the internet; the second is how server-side request forgery happens.
  • Outbound access for Google Speech and the Gemini proxy goes through a NAT gateway, or the instance keeps a public IP with no inbound rules at all. Both are safe for inbound; the difference is ₹2,800 a month and egress control.
  • User media is deleted from the box as soon as the output is verified in R2. It is a shared machine and it should never accumulate customers' videos.

8.6 Data at rest and in transit

  • Cloudflare R2: buckets private with no public access and no r2.dev URL enabled, encrypted at rest by Cloudflare, lifecycle rules matching the retention columns in plans, and reachable only through the Worker binding or our worker's credentials. Trade-off worth naming: R2 does not offer a customer-managed KMS key the way S3 does, so you get provider-managed encryption rather than a key you rotate. For finished dubs of customer-supplied video, held 7 days, that is a reasonable exchange for removing every egress charge. If a customer ever contractually requires customer-managed keys, that specific account's outputs would need to go to S3 instead.
  • Legacy S3 note: account-level Block Public Access stays on and SSE-KMS with a customer-managed key, a bucket policy that denies any request not using TLS, versioning on, access logging on, lifecycle rules matching the retention columns in plans.
  • RDS: private subnet, not publicly accessible, KMS encrypted, rds.force_ssl so unencrypted connections are refused, automated backups with point-in-time recovery, deletion protection on.
  • In transit: TLS 1.2 or better everywhere, including inside the VPC to RDS.
  • Deletion: an account deletion request removes R2 objects, job rows and personal fields, leaving only the anonymised billing records the law requires.

8.7 Upload safety

  • The browser uploads straight to R2 with a presigned PUT valid 15 minutes, pinned to one object key, content length and content type. A 30-minute video never passes through the API server's memory or disk.
  • Duration and size are enforced server-side after upload with ffprobe, because a client can claim anything. Rejected before any GPU time is spent: anything longer than the plan's ceiling, anything that probes as audio-only when a video was promised, anything with no audio stream at all.
  • The user's filename is never used as a path. The object key is uploads/{user_id}/{upload_id}.mp4, generated by us. The original name is kept as a display label only. This is how path traversal happens and it is trivial to prevent.
  • The job_id sent to the box is generated by us and matches the box's own allowlist of alphanumerics, hyphens and underscores.

9. Payments with Razorpay

9.1 The flow

  1. The user picks a plan. Our API creates a Razorpay subscription or order and returns only the opaque id plus the public key_id.
  2. Razorpay's own hosted checkout takes the card or UPI details. They never reach our servers, which keeps us entirely out of PCI scope.
  3. Razorpay calls POST /api/billing/webhook. We verify the HMAC signature before parsing the body, record the event id in webhook_events, and ignore duplicates.
  4. Only then does the subscription become active. The browser is never believed about payment. A user who closes the tab mid-payment is still upgraded when the webhook lands; a user who forges a success call to our API achieves nothing.

9.2 The e-mandate ceiling — RESOLVED: annual is billed as a one-off

The constraint. RBI's consolidated Digital Payments E-Mandate Framework, 2026 gathers the earlier recurring-payment circulars into one rulebook and keeps a ₹15,000 per-transaction threshold for recurring e-mandates processed without additional factor authentication. It is a regulatory limit, not a Razorpay one, and it applies to recurring debits generally — UPI Autopay and card mandates alike, not only UPI. A higher ₹1 lakh no-AFA threshold exists for specific categories such as credit card bills, insurance premiums and mutual fund SIPs; video dubbing is not one of them.

One correction to how this was framed earlier. Above ₹15,000 a mandate is not refused outright — each debit simply requires the customer to authenticate again. For an unattended annual renewal that is worse than a hard failure: the charge does not go through unless the customer happens to act on an authentication prompt, so the renewal fails quietly and the subscription lapses without anyone deciding to cancel. Annual Pro clears the threshold by ₹610 today, and stops clearing it the moment Pro's monthly price passes about ₹1,562 or the discount narrows.

A one-time order is not a mandate, so the limit does not apply to it at any price — and the customer is present and authenticating deliberately at the moment of purchase, which is exactly when an authentication step belongs. What this costs, stated plainly: annual no longer renews itself. There is no mandate behind a one-time order, so nothing charges the customer when the year ends. That is a genuine trade, and it needs four small things rather than being waved through.
  1. Renewal reminders are now load-bearing. SES emails at 14 days, 3 days and on the expiry day, each linking to a pre-filled checkout. Without them annual churn is silent and total — the customer does not decide to leave, they simply lapse.
  2. A grace period. Keep access for 7 days past current_period_end before dropping to free, so somebody who pays a day late does not lose their dashboard or a job in flight. Store it as an explicit grace_until rather than inferring it, so support can see the real answer in one query.
  3. The webhook handler switches on event type. One-time orders confirm as payment.captured against an order_id; mandates confirm as subscription.charged. Both paths must stay idempotent on provider_payment_id, which is what makes a replayed webhook harmless (section 7).
  4. The schema has to admit both shapes. An annual row has no provider_subscription_id, and cancel_at is meaningless for it — there is nothing to cancel, it lapses. Add auto_renew boolean to subscriptions and set it false for annual, so "will this renew?" has one honest column to read instead of being inferred from a null.
Keep the per-plan eligibility check anyway. Have the API decide, per plan, whether an amount is mandate-eligible, and let checkout offer autopay only where a mandate can actually complete unattended. Monthly prices are nowhere near the threshold, so this is insurance rather than a fix — but it is a few lines, and it stops a future pricing change from quietly becoming a payments outage.

9.3 International customers

Accepting foreign cards on Razorpay is not on by default. It requires a separate request from the dashboard, a KYC-verified account, and approval by their banking partners; Razorpay's own documentation puts average activation for a payment method at around ten working days. More than 160 currencies are supported once live.

The practical consequence for the build order: apply for international activation now, in parallel with Phase 1, because it is a waiting period you cannot compress and it is not on the engineering critical path. 9.4 The rules that keep billing honest

  • Idempotency by provider event id, unique-constrained. Razorpay retries webhooks; retries must be free.
  • Signature verified before the body is parsed, and a signature failure is logged and alerted, because it is either a misconfiguration or someone probing.
  • Quota is never granted by the checkout callback, only by a verified webhook.
  • A failed or cancelled job writes no usage row. Partial work is not billed even when the GPU time was real.
  • Downgrades take effect at period end, upgrades immediately with the minutes prorated, and both are computed from the ledger rather than adjusted by hand.

10.2.2 The traps, and they are not small

1. A refund is a new ledger row, never an edit. usage_ledger is append-only for the reason given in 3.2: a decrementing counter cannot be reconciled or explained to a customer who disputes a charge. So a refund inserts a compensating row — negative minutes_charged, kind='refund', carrying the job_id, the admin's id and the reason. The original charge row is never touched.

Editing or deleting the original would make the balance correct and the history a lie, and you would lose the one thing that lets you answer "why is this number what it is" six months later.
2. Granting a subscription must not write to payments. It is the obvious shortcut and it quietly corrupts the earnings figure sitting on the same screen. A grant writes a subscriptions row with provider='manual' and auto_renew=false, and nothing else. payments records money that actually arrived from Razorpay — never an entitlement you handed out.
3. Tag your own dubs, and keep them out of revenue but inside cost. Admin jobs skip the quota check, so they must carry origin='admin' and billable=false. They are excluded from per-customer usage and from anything revenue-shaped — but they burn real GPU minutes, so they stay visible in the cost view. Untagged, they silently distort the cost-per-video figures section 15 is built on, and you would be measuring your own testing as customer demand.
4. Read the instance state from AWS, not from our own table. gpu_state.desired_state is what we intended; the admin needs what is true. Show both, and highlight it when they disagree — a box that is actually running while we believe it is stopped is precisely the bug that quietly bills ₹47 an hour, and a dashboard reading from our own table would hide it. Cache the AWS call for about 30 seconds so an open dashboard cannot rate-limit the account.
5. "Today" and "this week" need one fixed timezone. Pin them to Asia/Kolkata, defined once server-side, with the week starting Monday. Computed from the admin's browser instead, the same day's takings change depending on where you are when you look, and two people comparing notes will never agree.
6. Cash collected is not recurring revenue — label it. Since annual is now a one-off order (9.2), a single ₹14,390 payment lands as one row today but covers twelve months. The figure you asked for is cash collected, which is the right number for "did money arrive", so show that and name it that. Just do not let a good annual day be read as a new monthly baseline. Refunds subtract from it.
7. The admin panel is the deliberate exception to the rule that keeps users apart. Every other query in this system is scoped by the session's own user id (8.4). Admin routes take a target user id from the request — which is exactly the shape of the vulnerability that rule exists to prevent. So:
  • Every admin route checks role='admin' server-side, on every request, with no exceptions and nothing inferred from the client.
  • They live under their own /api/admin/ prefix so they can never be confused with a customer route during review.
  • No admin capability ships in the customer frontend bundle. Not hidden behind a flag, not disabled in the UI — absent from the build.
  • Granting subscriptions and issuing refunds require the admin's password again, and are rate limited. A stolen admin session must not be able to hand out a year of Pro in silence.

11.2 The runbook

  1. In the new account, create the Terraform state bucket and lock table, then terraform apply with the new variables. Infrastructure exists, empty.
  2. Create the secrets in the new account's Secrets Manager: new database password, new Razorpay keys, rotated GPU tokens and a fresh Google service account.
  3. Build and push the images from git to the new account's ECR.
  4. Run Alembic against the new database to create the schema.
  5. Freeze writes. Put the API in maintenance mode, stop accepting jobs, and let running jobs finish. Because every job runs start to finish without pausing, the longest you ever wait is one job — there are no half-finished jobs to reconcile.
  6. pg_dump the old database, pg_restore into the new one, then verify row counts table by table and spot-check a few users, jobs and ledger sums.
  7. Storage does not move at all. The buckets are in Cloudflare R2, not AWS, so changing AWS account leaves every video exactly where it is. Repoint nothing, copy nothing, verify nothing. Under the old S3 design this step was an aws s3 sync across accounts behind a temporary cross-account bucket policy, followed by verifying object counts and checksums — and objects were re-encrypted with the new account's KMS key on copy, which took real time on a large bucket. Moving delivery to R2 deleted this entire step.
  8. Create the GPU instance in the new account from the same AMI or a fresh build. This is the step to be most careful with. The patched backend must arrive byte-identical: verify the five pinned file sizes and MD5s from the handbook, and re-check both guarded invariants — the leadin marker count of 5 in api/routers/dub_generate.py, and services/speech_rate.py at exactly 13,073 bytes. Then render the canary video from section 2.6 and compare it against the stored reference before any customer traffic arrives.
  9. Point DNS at the new front door. Watch error rates.
  10. Keep the old account read-only for a fortnight before deleting anything.
Rehearse this once into a throwaway account before you need it. A migration plan that has never been executed is a hypothesis. The rehearsal also produces the real timings, which is what tells you how long the maintenance window has to be — and it is much better to discover a byte-mismatch in the patched backend on a Tuesday afternoon than on the day your credits expire.

Because six of those GPU minutes are boot regardless of length, back-to-back jobs are much cheaper per video than isolated ones — which is the argument for queueing free jobs behind paid ones in 3.4, and worth remembering when setting prices.

Decision: 250 GB. An extra ₹803 a month takes free space from 32 GB to about 132 GB — four times today's headroom — for under a third of what 500 GB would have cost. It clears the 79%-full problem outright while leaving the option to step up later if a real need appears, which is the correct shape of decision for a resource that can grow but never shrink.
Two properties of EBS that decide this.
  • You pay for provisioned size, 24/7, running or stopped. Unlike the GPU, which costs nothing while the instance is off, the disk bills every hour of every day. So this lands entirely on the fixed monthly cost — 500 GB would take the fixed floor from ₹4,000–7,500 to roughly ₹6,800–10,300, a 40–60% increase in what you pay on a day with no customers.
  • A volume can grow but never shrink. 500 GB is a one-way commitment to ₹2,809 a month forever. Stepping up later is easy; stepping down is not possible.

Why 250 GB rather than 500

With resume removed, the pipeline needs disk for one job at a time — two or three gigabytes. Today's 32 GB already covers that, and clearing the leftover test jobs makes it about 38. So 500 GB would buy roughly 350 GB with no identified use, at ₹48,150 a year — close to what two Pro subscribers pay across a whole year, spent on empty space.

Growing to something is still right, because the real risk is not job-sized: the container's writable layer is at 29 GB and grows on its own, the 24 GB swapfile shares the volume, and a full disk fails every job at once rather than one. 250 GB gives four times today's headroom for 29% of the price of 500 GB.

The reason to prefer the smaller step is asymmetry, not thrift. Growing later is a live, non-disruptive operation that takes minutes; shrinking is impossible. So the cost of under-provisioning is a short maintenance task, while the cost of over-provisioning is a bill you cannot ever reduce. When one direction is cheap to correct and the other is not, start at the smaller end. Revisit if free space trends below about 60 GB, or if you add a second concurrent job, lipsync, or a new model family.
